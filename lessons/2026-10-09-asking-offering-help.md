## Review — recall before reading (4 minutes)

Use these prompts only if you have practiced the planning or clarification chunks:

1. Ask whether a friend has time this afternoon.
2. You think a box belongs by the door. Confirm where to put it.

Say a response before looking at the answer key. Change the time or place and try again.

If these chunks are unfamiliar, skip this review or try with the English you already know. No earlier lesson is required, and the lesson library is not a record of what you have learned.

## Learn — make the help clear (4 minutes)

**Situation:** You are taking bags into your home. A friend is nearby. You need the door held open and one small bag carried inside. Later, you notice your friend carrying some books.

**Goal:** Ask for one clear action, respond to the answer, and offer help in return.

A **chunk** is a group of words you can remember and use together. A **favor** is a small helpful action someone does for you.

{% include chunk-cards.html %}

### Choose the right direction

- **Could you help me with this bag?** You need help.
- **Can I help you with those books?** You offer help.
- **Could you do me a favor and hold the door?** You ask for a small action and explain it immediately.

Use **with + a thing**: “with this box.” You can also say **help me + a verb**: “help me carry this box.” After **Could you**, use the base verb: “help,” not “helping.”

“Can you help me?” is a valid request. Adding the task makes it easier to answer. “Could you help me with this bag, please?” is a polite option; you do not need to use the most formal wording every time.

### Everyday and work examples

- At home: “Could you do me a favor and water the plants?”
- At work: “Could you help me with this form, please?”
- Helping a colleague: “Can I help you with those folders?”
- After an offer: “Yes, please. That would be great.”
- When a friend is busy: “No worries. I'll ask someone else.”

**No worries** is informal. “That's okay. Thanks anyway” is a more neutral option. A polite request still allows the other person to say no.

Read the five chunk examples once. Look away and explain which phrases ask, offer, accept, or respond to a refusal.

## Practice — ask, listen to the answer, respond (8 minutes)

Say your replies before writing. Spend about one minute on each short task and three minutes on the conversation. The times are a guide; focus on saying useful English.

### A. Change one detail

Make two versions of each frame. Name a real task or thing.

1. “Could you do me a favor and ______?”
2. “Could you help me with ______?”
3. “Can I help you with ______?”

### B. Retrieve from situations

Cover the examples. Say one reply for each situation:

1. Your hands are full. Ask a friend to hold the door.
2. You cannot carry both bags. Ask for help with one bag.
3. A friend is carrying several books. Offer help.
4. Someone offers to carry a bag, and you want the help. Accept warmly.
5. A friend says, “Sorry, I can't help right now.” Show that you understand and choose another plan.

### C. Reply, then explain the task

Your partner asks: “Would you like some help?”

Accept the offer and ask them to carry the small bag. After they ask, “Where should I put it?”, tell them to put it by the sofa. Answer the question they actually ask.

### D. Make it clear and natural

Improve these replies. Some are grammar errors; others are valid but need clearer details or a more respectful response.

1. “Could you helping me with this bag?”
2. “Can I help you with carry those books?”
3. “Could you do me a favor?” Then you wait without explaining what you need.
4. Your friend says they must leave. You reply, “No, you must help me now.”

### E. Try the whole conversation

Open [the bags-and-books scenario]({{ '/scenarios/asking-offering-help.html' | relative_url }}). Use the partner-only turns before opening the model dialogue. Ask for help, accept an offer, respect a limit, and offer help in return.

**Changed situation:** Your friend cannot carry anything, but they can hold the door briefly. Accept that smaller offer and say you will carry the bag yourself.

**Switch roles:** You are now the friend. Offer help, explain one limit, and ask for a small favor in return. Wait for your partner after each turn.

### F. Make it yours

Choose a safe, real or imaginary task at home or work. Say one specific request and one offer of help. Then imagine the other person is busy and respond politely. Use three target chunks across your replies. Keep private names and account details out of your examples.

## Exit task — use it in a new situation (4 minutes)

You are setting up a room for a small event. You need help putting cups on a table. Your partner is sorting paper signs.

Cover the lesson and speak one turn at a time:

1. Ask your partner for help with the cups. Name the action you need.
2. They say, “Sorry, I need to finish these signs first.” Respond without pressing them.
3. Offer help with the signs instead.
4. They reply, “Thanks. Could you hold this sign while I attach it?” Accept and say what you will do.

**Try a second version:** Your partner offers to help with the cups after finishing the signs. Accept warmly and explain where the cups go.

Use at least three of the five target chunks across both versions. Choose phrases that fit the turn; do not force every chunk into one reply.

### Delayed recall

Talk about breakfast for one minute. Then, without notes, recall a phrase for each purpose: asking a small favor, requesting specific help, offering help, accepting help, and responding when someone cannot help.

### Check your attempt

- Was the request clear enough for someone to act on?
- Did you keep “you help me” and “I help you” in the right direction?
- Did you respond to the other person's actual answer and respect their limit?
- Could you use at least three target chunks without reading them?

If you needed a hint, record that honestly. Retry with a different task before deciding whether the language feels independent.

**Remember:** Name the task. Leave room for a no. Respond to the answer. Offer help when you can.

## Answer key and sample responses

All open-ended responses below are samples, not the only correct answers. Different tasks and natural wording are valid when they meet the goal.

### Review — sample responses

1. “Are you free this afternoon?” A changed time: “Are you free tomorrow morning?”
2. “Just to check, this box goes by the door, right?” A changed place: “Just to check, it goes by the sofa, right?”

### Learn — explain the chunks

- “Could you do me a favor?” introduces a small request. Explain the action next, or add “and + action.”
- “Could you help me with ...?” asks for help with a specific task or thing.
- “Can I help you with ...?” offers help to someone else.
- “Yes, please. That would be great” accepts an offer warmly.
- “No worries” tells a friend their refusal or small problem is okay.

**Look-away examples:** “Could you do me a favor and open the window?” / “Could you help me with this form?” / “Can I help you with dinner?” / “Yes, please. That would be great.” / “No worries. I'll ask someone else.”

### Practice A — two sample versions

1. “Could you do me a favor and hold the door?” / “Could you do me a favor and water the plants?”
2. “Could you help me with this bag?” / “Could you help me with this form?”
3. “Can I help you with those books?” / “Can I help you with dinner?”

### Practice B — sample replies

1. “Could you do me a favor and hold the door?”
2. “Could you help me with this bag, please?”
3. “Can I help you with those books?”
4. “Yes, please. That would be great.”
5. “No worries. I'll ask someone else.”

### Practice C — one possible exchange

**Friend:** Would you like some help?  
**You:** Yes, please. That would be great. Could you carry the small bag?

**Friend:** Where should I put it?  
**You:** By the sofa, please. Thanks for your help.

### Practice D — corrections and clearer choices

| Type | You said | Better | Why | One reusable example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Must fix | Could you helping me with this bag? | Could you help me with this bag? | Use the base verb after could you. | Could you help me with this form? |
| Must fix | Can I help you with carry those books? | Can I help you carry those books? | Use a base verb after help you, or say with carrying. | Can I help you carry this bag? |
| Natural upgrade | Could you do me a favor? Then no details. | Could you do me a favor and hold the door? | The original question is valid, but the person needs the task before deciding. | Could you do me a favor and check this sentence? |
| Natural upgrade | No, you must help me now. | No worries. I'll ask someone else. | The grammar is possible, but it pressures someone who has said they must leave. | That's okay. Thanks anyway. |

**Optional style:** “Can you help me with this bag?” is also valid. “Could you ...?” is a polite alternative, not a required correction every time.

### Practice E — conversation, change, and role reversal

The [scenario]({{ '/scenarios/asking-offering-help.html' | relative_url }}) contains a ten-turn model and sample responses for the change and role reversal.

**Changed situation — sample:** “Yes, please. That would be great. Could you hold the door? I'll carry the bag myself.”

**Switch roles — sample:** Say “Can I help you with that bag?” and wait. If they ask about the box, say “Sorry, I don't have time to move the box.” Later, ask “Could you do me a favor and hold the door?” and wait again. Respond to what your partner actually says.

### Practice F — sample turns

**Request:** “Could you help me with this form, please?”  
**After a refusal:** “No worries. I'll ask someone else.”  
**Offer in another situation:** “Can I help you with those folders?”

### Exit task — one possible exchange

**You:** Could you help me with the cups, please? Could you put them on the table?

**Friend:** Sorry, I need to finish these signs first.  
**You:** No worries. Can I help you with the signs?

**Friend:** Thanks. Could you hold this sign while I attach it?  
**You:** Of course. I'll hold it here.

**Second version — sample:**

**Friend:** I can help with the cups after I finish the signs.  
**You:** Yes, please. That would be great. They go on the table by the door.

### Delayed recall — sample chunks

Could you do me a favor? / Could you help me with ...? / Can I help you with ...? / Yes, please. That would be great. / No worries.

**Sample break activity:** “I had toast for breakfast. I drank some tea. Tomorrow, I might make eggs.” Your own simple description is also valid.

**Success criteria:** The request names the task, the offer has the right speaker direction, the refusal is respected, and at least three target chunks fit the exchanges without notes. Reading these samples is preparation for another attempt, not proof of mastery.
